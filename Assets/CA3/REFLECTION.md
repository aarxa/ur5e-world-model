## 2. How does your World Model work? What data is collected, what is your model architecture, and what does your model predict?

`RolloutRecorder` logged 70,000 transitions in Unity at a 0.01 s fixed timestep. Each row holds the joint angles `q`, the joint velocities `qd`, the commanded joint targets `q*`, and the resulting `q'` and `qd'`. The actions came from `ArmJointController`, which picked a random goal around the UR5e's home pose every 1.5 s and sent it straight to the position servos. The model is the `WorldModel` class in `train.py`: an MLP with three 256-unit SiLU layers. It takes the normalized `[q, qd, q*]` and also rebuilds the servo error `q* − q` internally, because the actuators produce torque from exactly that difference. It predicts the change in state `s' − s`, and it is trained with a one-step loss plus a multi-step loss that feeds its own predictions back for up to 16 steps. On held-out data it reached 0.009 rad error one step ahead and about 0.11 rad after 50–100 open-loop steps, which I could see in Unity as the ghost arm slowly drifting away from the real arm until it reanchored.

## 3. How does your controller work? How is it trained, how does it connect to your world model, and what does it control?

My controller is a linear policy, `a = goal + W·[q − goal, qd] + b`. Its output goes through a low-pass filter and a 0.05 rad-per-step rate limit, then becomes the six joint position targets sent to the UR5e's servos. It is trained with CMA-ES entirely inside the world model: each set of 78 parameters is scored on 16 imagined 200-step episodes, each moving from a random rest pose to a random goal, with a reward that penalizes joint error, velocity and jerky actions. In my run the best held-out imagined return, −3.257, belonged to the θ = 0 baseline, which is plain goal commanding, so the script kept that. When I looked into it, I found that with stiff servos and the smoothing in the loop the policy has almost no room to improve, and hand-picked gains ranked the same way in imagination as in real MuJoCo. In Unity, `WorldModelController` runs this policy every `FixedUpdate`, and in the Step 3 check the arm moved smoothly to each random goal.

## 4. What does your IK solver do? How does it work, and how does it connect to your controller? Why and how did you pick this algorithm?

`IkGoalProvider` turns the position of the `TrackingTarget` sphere into six joint angles and passes them to `ArmJointController.SetTarget`, which makes them the goal the learned controller tracks. It uses damped least squares. Each iteration measures the end-effector error, builds a 3×6 Jacobian by nudging each joint and rerunning MuJoCo's `mj_kinematics`, and takes the step `Jᵀ(JJᵀ + λ²I)⁻¹e` with λ = 0.05. A position target only constrains 3 of the UR5e's 6 joints, so a null-space term pulls the extra freedom toward the home pose. Solutions are also clamped to the joint region the rollouts covered, so the world model never sees poses it wasn't trained on. I picked damped least squares because the damping keeps it stable near singularities and for targets out of reach, and the finite-difference Jacobian always matches the imported model without any Unity/MuJoCo coordinate conversion. When I dragged the target past the arm's reach, the arm simply stretched toward it and the gizmo turned red, instead of the solver blowing up.

## 5. How has your understanding of World Models changed while doing this assignment?

Before this assignment I thought of a world model mainly as a next-step predictor, where being accurate one step ahead was the whole goal. The numbers changed that. An error of 0.009 rad one step ahead grew to about 0.11 rad after 50 steps, and the ghost arm needed regular reanchoring, so what matters for planning is how error compounds over a rollout. That is why training with a multi-step loss mattered. I also learned that a controller optimized in imagination can exploit the model's mistakes: a policy trained on a weaker model scored better in imagination but tracked worse on the real arm. Finally, building knowledge of the actuators into the inputs, the `q* − q` error, helped more than simply making the network bigger.

## 6. How might you implement what you have learned here in your Term Project?

The term project, Digital Roommate, is a Unity apartment where a roommate uses a cup, leaves it on the table, and cleans it up while you are away, driven by a behavior tree and a JSON world-state record. The most direct carry-over is reaching. The roommate currently uses Two Bone IK, and the damped-least-squares approach from `IkGoalProvider`, with a target transform on the cup, a null-space pull toward a natural pose and limits on joint ranges, would make reaching more reliable across the randomized cup and table positions. The ghost arm also maps onto the "state match" metric: just as the driver reanchors when its prediction drifts from the real arm, the roommate should compare its recorded world state with the scene before each action, and re-plan when they disagree, for example when the cup was moved while you were away. If I reach the stretch goal of physics grasping, the MuJoCo plugin and a small learned model of the grasp could predict whether a pickup will succeed before the roommate commits to it, which targets the failed-pickups metric. CA3 also taught me to keep learning where the uncertainty is and use exact tools like NavMesh and IK where the geometry is known, which fits the plan to keep the planner simple and state-driven.
